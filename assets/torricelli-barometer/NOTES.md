# Notes: Torricelli's experiment (টরিসেলির পরীক্ষা)

Book: physics-9-10 › 5.4.1 › p145–146. Screenshot: `reference/page-145.png` (both pages). The owner's pictures:
`reference/owner-barometer.png` (the main one: a tube in a dish of mercury on a wooden table, the vacuum, 760 mm,
an atmospheric-pressure arrow on the dish) and `reference/owner-jet.png` (a tank with water height H, a hole at
depth h, the jet's range R: Torricelli's law of efflux).

## What the page shows
- **The straw (p145):** a straw works because of the air's pressure. A 10.5 m straw would lift a cold drink only to
  10.3 m however hard you suck (the book takes a cold drink to be about as dense as water). A mercury straw stops at
  76 cm, because mercury is 13.6 times as dense as water. A straw held in the bottle without sucking: the air in the
  mouth and over the drink press the same, no net force (কার্যকর বল), nothing rises. Sucking lowers the mouth's
  pressure and the air over the drink pushes it up.
- **Torricelli, 1643 (p146, চিত্র ৫.১০):** a tube closed at one end, filled with mercury, turned over into a dish of
  mercury. The mercury falls and stops at exactly 76 cm, leaving a vacuum at the top (the emptiness sucking tries to
  make). The air presses on the mercury in the dish; the pressure passes through the liquid to the bottom of the
  tube. Nothing presses from the closed top. So the pressure of the 76 cm column balances the air's pressure. The
  instrument is the ব্যারোমিটার, still made this way.

## The one idea to teach
The air's pressure holds up a column of liquid: 76 cm of mercury (or 10.3 m of water), because P = hρg of that
column equals the air's pressure, about 10⁵ N/m² (topic 5.4), when nothing presses from above.

## What the student does (one row of words, the book's order)
1. **স্ট্র**: চুমুক দাও / চুমুক থামাও. Equal blue arrows (outside air, mouth air) → nothing rises; sucking shortens
   the mouth's arrow (the difference is exaggerated, said in the note) and the drink climbs to the mouth.
2. **টরিসেলি** (the owner's main picture): four numbered steps: fill the tube from a beaker, seal it with a thumb and
   turn it over into the dish, take the thumb away (the mercury falls, bobs, stops at 76 cm; the camera visits the
   vacuum and the dish), pierce the top (air rushes in, the column falls into the dish).
3. **হেলাও**: the tube tilts 10° a step to 40°; the top stays level with 76 on the scale, the mercury fills more of
   the tube (L = 76/cos θ), a thin vacuum is still left at 40° (tube 105 cm inside).
4. **পাহাড়ে**: 0, 1000, 2000, 3000 m; the window's view changes; about 76, 67, 60, 53 cm (the standard atmosphere,
   scaled to the book's 76 cm at the sea; "প্রায়" on screen). Air arrows shorten in proportion.
5. **পানি**: the schoolyard; a glass pipe in a tub fills with water to 10.3 m, nearly to the roof of a three-storey
   school, beside a small 76 cm mercury barometer and a red-and-white measuring staff.
6. **ছিদ্র**: the owner's third picture (below).

The panel works out P = hρg with each example's numbers (book: ρ of mercury 13,600 kg m⁻³ = 13.6 × water, g = 9.8):
0.76 × 13,600 × 9.8 = 1,01,293 Pa ≈ 10⁵ N/m²; 10.3 × 1,000 × 9.8 = 1,00,940 Pa.

## How the third picture (the jet, Torricelli's law) is blended in
- The book does not teach the efflux speed v = √(2gh), so no formula from it appears. What is used is what the page
  needs: liquid pressure grows with depth (P = hρg, topic 5.3), and a liquid is held in when nothing presses from
  above.
- **The tank (ছিদ্র):** a glass tank of water with a lid and one corked hole near the bottom, laid out like the
  picture (h, the depth of the hole under the surface, is marked; the jet lands on the bench). Lid on, cork out: a
  few drops, then it stops; a blue arrow shows the outside air pressing on the hole ("just as it holds the mercury in
  Torricelli's tube"). Lid off: the air presses on the water's surface too, the column hρg above the hole pushes a
  jet out, and as the tank drains (shown faster) h falls and the jet weakens. The jet is drawn on its true parabola
  (reach 2√(h·fall)), but only "h বেশি, ধারা জোরে" is said.
- **The pierced tube (Torricelli's step 4)** is the brief's first example, kept to what is true.

### A correction to the brief
The brief suggested that a hole in the barometer tube *below* the mercury's top would make mercury spurt out, harder
the lower the hole. That is not so: inside the column the pressure at height y above the dish is P_air − yρg, less
than the air outside, everywhere above the dish's surface. A hole anywhere in the tube above the dish lets air **in**
(bubbles rise and the column falls); mercury would spurt only from a hole below the dish's surface (in the dish's
wall), which would spill mercury on a school bench. So the figure pierces the top only, and the jet lives in the
water tank.

mercury and the glass read against it), a green window with an iron grille and marigolds. Mercury is mirror-bright

(note on the close views).
